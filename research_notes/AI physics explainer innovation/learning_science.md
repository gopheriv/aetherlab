# Learning Science and Education Research Relevant to AI Physics Explainers, Animations, Simulations and AI Tutoring

Research date: 2026-09-23. Scope: evidence on what makes students learn physics better, and what that means for an AI physics workbench (derivations, narrated animated lessons, transcripts, quizzes, study-guide PDFs, parametric slider canvas).

Evidence-strength legend used below: [META] meta-analysis; [RCT] randomized trial; [QUASI] quasi-experimental or observational; [INDUSTRY] vendor or self-published, not peer reviewed; [WP] working paper or preprint.

---

## 1. Mayer's multimedia learning principles: evidence and what it means for AI-narrated animations

### Takeaway
Mayer's principles come from many small lab experiments with consistently medium-to-large median effects (d of about 0.4 to 1.3). They map directly onto how an AI-narrated animation should be built: cut the decoration, narrate instead of printing captions, cue what matters, break the lesson into learner-paced segments, pre-teach key terms, and use a conversational voice. The effects are real but come mostly from short, immediate-transfer lab studies, so expect smaller effects in the field.

### Cited Findings
- Median effect sizes Mayer reports for the principles that reduce extraneous processing: coherence d = 0.70, signaling d = 0.46, redundancy d = 0.87, spatial contiguity d = 0.79, temporal contiguity d = 1.30. For managing essential processing: segmenting d = 0.70, pre-training d = 0.46, modality d = 0.72. For fostering generative processing: personalization d = 0.79 [META-style review of Mayer lab experiments]. See [Mayer 2017, "Using multimedia for e-learning", J. Computer Assisted Learning](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jcal.12197) and [Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning, ch. 12](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/CD5B7AE1279A9AB81F8EEBB53DBEC86E).
- Another summary gives coherence as supported in 23 of 23 experimental tests with a median d = 0.86. That differs from the 0.70 above because it comes from a different edition or count, so the exact number depends on which version is cited — [ResearchGate: Principles for Reducing Extraneous Processing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/262915119_Principles_for_reducing_extraneous_processing_in_multimedia_learning_Coherence_signaling_redundancy_spatial_contiguity_and_temporal_contiguity_principles).
- Definitions as Mayer states them. Coherence: exclude extraneous material. Signaling: highlight the essential material. Redundancy: do not add on-screen text that repeats narration over narrated graphics — [same source](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/262915119_Principles_for_reducing_extraneous_processing_in_multimedia_learning_Coherence_signaling_redundancy_spatial_contiguity_and_temporal_contiguity_principles); [Managing Essential Processing chapter](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/359588534_Principles_for_Managing_Essential_Processing_in_Multimedia_Learning).
- The expertise reversal effect limits all of this: support that helps novices can hurt more knowledgeable learners. Kalyuga's narrative review of 26 studies found differences between low- and high-prior-knowledge learners of d = 0.45 to 2.99. A 2025 meta-analysis confirms that lower-prior-knowledge learners benefit more from high-assistance instruction and higher-prior-knowledge learners benefit more from low assistance — [Learning and Instruction 2025 meta-analysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475225000660); [Chartered College summary](https://my.chartered.college/impact_article/expertise-reversal-effect-and-its-instructional-implications/).

### Inferences
- **Redundancy versus transcripts.** Do not burn the full narration into the video as captions by default. A separate transcript, or captions the viewer switches on, is fine and is needed for accessibility. Short on-screen labels next to the relevant object (spatial contiguity) are fine. What hurts learning is full sentences on screen that duplicate the audio.
- **Coherence.** The planner and LLM tend to add decorative visuals, background music, "fun fact" asides and dramatic transitions. These should be removed or kept minimal. This argues for a rule in the scene generator or verifier that rejects non-essential decoration.
- **Signaling.** Highlight the variable or vector being discussed at the moment the narration names it (temporal contiguity, d = 1.30, the largest effect in the set). The narration timestamps should drive the Manim highlight and indicate calls.
- **Segmenting.** Break the hook, intuition, math, worked example, recap sequence into chapters with a pause or continue control. Segmenting helps because the learner controls the pace, not just because chapters exist.
- **Pre-training.** A short "key symbols and terms" card before the math segment (for example, what ω, k and φ mean) is a low-cost way to apply pre-training (d = 0.46).
- **Personalization.** Use a conversational narration style ("you", "let's") rather than a formal textbook voice (d = 0.79).
- **Expertise reversal.** Offer a "skip the intuition / I know this" path, or adapt to a short pre-quiz, so advanced users are not slowed by scaffolding they no longer need.

### Gaps
- I did not find a recent (2020+) independent meta-analysis that re-estimates each Mayer principle with correction for publication bias. The effect sizes above come from Mayer's own summaries of mostly lab studies with immediate tests. Effects in classrooms over time are likely smaller, but I did not find a source that quantifies how much.
- The generative activity principle (learning by drawing, explaining or teaching) did not come up in my searches with a specific effect size; see the self-explanation and retrieval evidence in Section 4 instead.
- I found no studies of AI-generated (as opposed to hand-authored) narrated animations tested against Mayer's principles.

## 2. Animations versus static diagrams, interactive simulations (PhET), and the illusion of understanding from passive video

### Takeaway
Animations beat static pictures by a small-to-medium amount on average (d = 0.37), and by more when the animation shows the actual process rather than decorating it. Well-designed interactive simulations can equal or beat real lab equipment for conceptual learning. The biggest risk for a video-first product is fluency: clear, polished explainer videos make students feel they understand without making them understand, and a video that plainly shows the correct concept is weaker than one that states the common misconception and then refutes it (effect sizes about 0.8).

### Cited Findings
- **Animation versus static.** Höffler & Leutner (2007) meta-analysis of 26 studies (76 comparisons): mean weighted d = 0.37 (95% CI 0.25–0.49) for dynamic over static visuals. The effect was larger when the animation was representational rather than decorative (d = 0.40), highly realistic or video-based (d = 0.76), and when procedural-motor knowledge was the target (d = 1.06) [META] — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475207001077); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/248498154_Instructional_animation_versus_static_pictures_A_meta-analysis).
- A later meta-analysis on the same question (Berney & Bétrancourt 2016, "Does animation enhance learning?") exists — [TECFA PDF](https://tecfa.unige.ch/perso/sandra/pdf/Earli2016_berney_betrancourt_FINAL.pdf) — but I could not retrieve its effect sizes (see Gaps).
- **PhET simulation versus real equipment.** Finkelstein et al. (2005, PRST-PER) replaced real DC-circuit equipment with the PhET Circuit Construction Kit in half the lab sections. Simulation students built real circuits faster, did better on a challenge question right after the lab, and did better on DC-circuit final exam questions two months later [QUASI/field experiment] — [SERC/Carleton summary](https://serc.carleton.edu/sp/library/phet/examples/example2.html).
- Across contexts, substituting the Circuit Construction Kit for real equipment gave better conceptual learning in the best cases and the same conceptual learning in the worst cases — [SERC/Carleton](https://serc.carleton.edu/sp/library/phet/examples/example2.html).
- **PhET design and guidance findings** (Wieman, Adams, Perkins 2008, *Science* 322:682) and related work on "what levels of guidance promote engaged exploration". The guidelines are: define specific learning goals, encourage sense-making and reasoning, connect to prior knowledge and real-world experience, **do not over-constrain exploration**, and require reasoning across multiple representations — [Science](https://www.science.org/doi/abs/10.1126/science.1161948); [PhET research page](https://phet.colorado.edu/en/research); [Teaching physics using PhET (TPT)](https://phet.colorado.edu/publications/Teaching_physics_using_PhET_TPT.pdf).
- **Simulations meta-analysis (K-12 STEM).** D'Angelo et al. (2014, SRI): 55 studies of K-12 science (1991–2012). On average simulations had a positive effect on science learning [META] — [SRI full report](https://www.sri.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/simulations-for-stem-learning-full-report.pdf); [SRI brief](https://www.sri.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/simulations-for-stem-learning-brief.pdf). (The pooled g could not be verified in this session; see Gaps.)
- **Muller (Veritasium PhD): misconceptions-first video.** 364 first-year physics students were randomized to four multimedia treatments on Newton's first and second laws: Exposition, Extended Exposition, Refutation (states the common misconceptions and refutes them), and Dialogue (student–tutor discussion). Compared with Exposition, Refutation gave effect size 0.79 and Dialogue 0.83. Low-prior-knowledge students benefited most, and high-prior-knowledge students were not disadvantaged. Students reported investing more mental effort in the misconception-based videos, and mental effort correlated with learning [RCT] — [Muller, Sharma & Reimann 2008, J. Computer Assisted Learning, "Saying the wrong thing"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1365-2729.2007.00248.x); [Muller PhD thesis](https://www.academia.edu/18111353/PhD_Muller_Designing_effective_multimedia_for_physics_education); [Raising cognitive load with linear multimedia to promote conceptual change](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229527453_Raising_cognitive_load_with_linear_multimedia_to_promote_conceptual_change).
- **Illusion of understanding (Kulgemeyer & Wittwer 2022/2023).** 149 physics learners watched either a force video containing misconceptions or a scientifically correct video matched for comprehensibility and length. The misconception group reported **the same perceived understanding** but acquired more misconceptions and less scientific knowledge. The authors conclude explainer videos should not be used as standalone learning tools [RCT] — [Int. J. Science & Math Education](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10763-022-10265-7); [PMC full text](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8932681/).
- Follow-ups by the same group compare explainer videos with written explanations on the illusion of understanding (2022), and test **integrating learning tasks after explainer videos** (2026) — [IJSE 2022](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09500693.2022.2100507); [IJSE 2026](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09500693.2026.2646710). I did not retrieve the effect sizes of the 2026 paper.
- **Feeling of learning versus actual learning (Deslauriers et al. 2019, PNAS).** In Harvard intro physics, with random assignment and identical content, students in active classes learned more but *felt* they learned less than students in polished passive lectures from highly rated instructors. The authors warn that judging instruction by students' perceived learning can favor inferior passive methods [RCT/crossover] — [PNAS PDF](https://www.pnas.org/doi/pdf/10.1073/pnas.1821936116); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31484770/).

### Inferences
- **An error in an AI-generated video is doubly harmful.** Kulgemeyer & Wittwer show that a wrong explanation leaves learners just as confident as a correct one. An LLM physics error in a smooth Manim video is therefore worse than a text error, because the polish hides it. This strongly supports AetherLab's physics verifier and numeric grounding work, and it argues for flagging when a claim has not been verified.
- **Lesson template: add a misconception beat.** The current arc is hook → intuition → math → worked example → recap. Muller's data (d ≈ 0.8) suggests the hook, or a slot right after it, should state the most common wrong belief for the topic (for example, "a force is needed to keep it moving"), predict with it, and then refute it. The misconception can come from a curated physics-misconception list (for example, FCI item categories) rather than being invented by the LLM.
- **Dialogue format.** The student–tutor Dialogue treatment was the best condition (d = 0.83). A two-voice narration mode (a curious learner voicing naive ideas and a tutor answering) is a cheap variant grounded in evidence.
- **Never ship video alone.** Every video should end in, and ideally be interrupted by, a retrieval or prediction task (Section 4). This is the main defense against the fluency illusion.
- **Slider canvas: guided but not over-constrained.** The PhET guidelines imply the slider canvas should come with a goal or challenge ("find the angle that maximizes range", "make the two oscillators beat at 2 Hz") and prompt the student to predict before moving a slider, while still allowing free exploration.
- **Do not use in-app satisfaction ratings as the success metric.** Deslauriers shows students' feeling of learning is anti-correlated with active methods. Measure learning with quiz performance, ideally delayed.

### Gaps
- Pooled effect sizes for D'Angelo et al. (2014) and Berney & Bétrancourt (2016) were not retrieved; the full PDFs above would give them.
- I found no RCT that directly compares a narrated animation plus live parametric sliders against the narrated animation alone. This is a product experiment AetherLab could run.
- I found no direct evidence on whether AI-generated physics animations cause more or fewer misconceptions than human-authored ones.

## 3. Physics Education Research: misconceptions, interactive engagement, peer instruction, predict–observe–explain, worked examples and fading, productive failure

### Takeaway
Physics Education Research's strongest result is that interactive engagement roughly doubles conceptual gains on the Force Concept Inventory compared with traditional lecture (Hake normalized gain 0.48 vs 0.23). Active learning across STEM raises exam scores by about 0.47 SD and cuts failure rates. Worked examples help novices (g ≈ 0.48) but should be faded as expertise grows. Letting students attempt a problem before instruction (productive failure) improves conceptual understanding and transfer (d ≈ 0.36) without hurting procedural skill.

### Cited Findings
- **Hake (1998), 6,542 students in 62 intro physics courses.** Traditional courses (14 courses, N = 2,084) had an average normalized FCI/MD gain of ⟨g⟩ = 0.23 ± 0.04. Interactive-engagement courses (48 courses, N = 4,458) had ⟨g⟩ = 0.48 ± 0.14, about double. Normalized gain = (post − pre)/(100 − pre) [QUASI, large survey; not randomized, and instructors self-selected] — [ERIC ED441679](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED441679); [UBC PDF](https://applied-science-cisdev.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2017/03/Hake_1998_Normalized_gain.pdf); [Hake 2002 "Lessons from the physics-education reform effort", arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0106087).
- Normalized gain g and Cohen's d can disagree, and g has known biases related to pretest level — [arXiv 1612.09180, comparison of Hake's g and Cohen's d](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1612.09180) (flag: g is not an effect size in the usual sense).
- **Freeman et al. (2014, PNAS), 225 STEM studies.** Exam and concept inventory performance rose 0.47 SD under active learning (n = 158 studies). Students under traditional lecturing were 1.5× more likely to fail (odds ratio 1.95, n = 67 studies) [META] — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1319030111). A follow-up found active learning narrows achievement gaps for underrepresented students — [Theobald et al. 2020, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1916903117).
- **Peer instruction (Crouch & Mazur 2001, AJP).** Ten years of Harvard data. Normalized gains were regularly about twice those of traditional lecture, reaching ⟨g⟩ = 0.74 in the sixth year of implementation. Both conceptual reasoning and quantitative problem solving improved [QUASI, single-institution] — [AJP](https://pubs.aip.org/aapt/ajp/article/69/9/970/310529/Peer-Instruction-Ten-years-of-experience-and); [MIT-hosted PDF](https://web.mit.edu/jbelcher/www/TEALref/Crouch_Mazur.pdf); [PhysPort research summary](https://www.physport.org/methods/section.cfm?G=Peer_Instruction&S=Research).
- **Worked examples (Barbieri et al. 2023, Educational Psychology Review), 55 studies and 181 effect sizes in mathematics.** Average g = 0.48. Moderators examined were correct versus incorrect examples, pairing with self-explanation prompts, and timing [META] — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1); [author PDF](https://www.danamillercotto.com/uploads/4/7/7/2/47725475/barbieri_et_al__2023__we_meta-analysis.pdf).
- **Fading.** Gradually removing worked steps triggers self-explanation and manages the move from studying examples to solving problems, which counters expertise reversal — [Salden et al., expertise reversal and worked examples in tutored problem solving](http://www.cee.uma.pt/ron/Salden%20et%20al.%20-%20The%20Expertise%20Reversal%20Effect%20and%20Worked%20Examples.pdf); [Wikipedia: worked-example effect (overview)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worked-example_effect).
- **Erroneous examples.** The order and congruency of correct and erroneous worked examples affect learning and cognitive load — [PMC9648051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9648051/).
- **Productive failure (Sinha & Kapur 2021, Review of Educational Research).** 53 studies, 166 comparisons, more than 12,000 participants. Problem solving before instruction beat instruction-first on conceptual knowledge and transfer (d = 0.36, 95% CI 0.20–0.51) without compromising procedural knowledge [META] — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543211019105); [PDF](https://janfasen.nl/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Sinha-and-Kapur-PS-I.pdf). The effect depends on fidelity: the problem-solving phase must be designed to surface students' prior ideas and must be followed by instruction that builds on them.
- **Misconceptions.** Misconceptions close to everyday experience are more attractive than correct explanations — [Kulgemeyer & Wittwer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10763-022-10265-7). Cognitive dissonance versus p-prims (knowledge-in-pieces) approaches to conceptual change remain debated — [arXiv 2308.15601](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.15601); [arXiv 2405.20923, review of misconceptions in university physics](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.20923) (flag: contested theoretical area).

### Inferences
- **Build predict–observe–explain into the product.** Before a video plays the key animation, or before a slider change reveals the result, ask a committed prediction question: multiple choice built from common misconception distractors, as with FCI items. Then show the outcome and ask for a one-line explanation. This combines peer-instruction-style concept questions, productive failure and Muller's refutation approach. POE as a named method did not come up with a pooled effect size in this session (see Gaps), but its parts are each supported.
- **"Try it first" mode for problems.** Offer an optional mode where the student attempts the problem, or a variant of it, before the worked example appears (productive failure, d = 0.36 on conceptual and transfer outcomes).
- **Faded worked examples.** Generate a sequence: a full worked example, then the same structure with the last step blank, then two steps blank, and so on. The existing numeric solver can check the student's filled-in steps.
- **Erroneous examples.** An "find the error in this derivation" exercise, which the LLM can produce by deliberately injecting a common error, is a natural fit for derivations. Correct examples should come first for novices.
- **Measurement.** If AetherLab wants credible efficacy claims, it can administer FCI-style items before and after and report normalized gain alongside Cohen's d.

### Gaps
- I did not find a pooled meta-analytic effect size specifically for predict–observe–explain in physics in this session.
- I found no randomized evidence on peer instruction delivered by an AI peer instead of human peers.

## 4. Retrieval practice, spaced repetition, interleaving and self-explanation

### Takeaway
These are among the best-replicated effects in learning science. Practice testing beats restudying. Spacing improves long-term retention, and the best gap between sessions grows with how long you need to remember. Interleaving mixed problem types gives g ≈ 0.42. Prompting self-explanation gives g ≈ 0.55. All four are cheap for an app to implement and directly counter the passive-video fluency trap.

### Cited Findings
- **Practice testing (Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan 2017, Review of Educational Research, 217+ studies).** Practice tests were more beneficial than restudying and all other comparison conditions. The advantage was consistent over restudy and much larger against no-activity or filler controls. Classroom effects were comparable to lab effects, secondary students showed the largest effects, and feedback added only a small extra benefit [META] — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0034654316689306); [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1141817); [Learning Scientists summary](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/2/9-1). (Overall g was not verified in this session; see Gaps.)
- **Spacing (Cepeda et al. 2006, Psychological Bulletin).** 839 assessments in 317 experiments across 184 articles. The inter-study interval and the retention interval jointly determine retention: the best spacing gap increases as the desired retention interval increases [META] — [Cepeda author page](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/CPVWR2006.html); [PDF](https://augmentingcognition.com/assets/Cepeda2006.pdf). Flag: mostly verbal-recall tasks, not physics problem solving.
- **Interleaving (Brunmair & Richter 2019, Psychological Bulletin).** 59 studies, 238 effect sizes, 158 samples. Overall Hedges' g = 0.42, larger for visual materials such as paintings (g = 0.67). The key moderator was similarity: interleaving helps most when the categories are similar and easily confused [META] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/); [author PDF](https://www.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf).
- **Self-explanation (Bisra et al. 2018, Educational Psychology Review).** 64 reports, 69 effect sizes, about 6,000 participants. Random-effects g = 0.55 for self-explanation prompts during study or problem solving, robust across many moderators [META] — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x); [gwern PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf); [BPS summary](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique-according-meta-analysis-64-studies).
- Pairing worked examples with self-explanation prompts is a moderator studied in the worked-examples meta-analysis — [Barbieri et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1).

### Inferences
- **Quizzes should be retrieval practice, not an afterthought.** Put 1–2 recall or prediction questions between video segments, not only at the end. This also applies segmenting from Section 1.
- **Spaced review queue.** Store every quiz item the student has seen and resurface missed and correct items at expanding intervals (for example 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 3 weeks), since the best gap grows with the retention goal. A desktop app can do this locally; a "Review due today" panel is the simplest version.
- **Interleaving for physics.** Interleaving helps most when categories are confusable, which in physics means problem types students mix up: energy conservation vs momentum conservation, static vs kinetic friction, series vs parallel circuits. Mixed quizzes across a student's past topics, where the student must first choose the principle, are a direct application.
- **Self-explanation prompts.** After each worked-example step, ask "Why is this step valid?" or "Which principle justified this line?" with a short free-text answer the LLM evaluates. Given the g = 0.55 effect, this is one of the most valuable things the tutor chat can do.
- **Study-guide PDF.** Make it a retrieval tool, not a summary: questions on one side and answers on the other or at the back, a "cover and recall" layout, and a spaced self-test schedule.

### Gaps
- Exact pooled effect sizes from Adesope et al. (2017) could not be verified in this session. Commonly cited figures (overall g of about 0.5–0.6) should be checked against the full paper before use.
- I found no physics-specific spaced-repetition RCT in this session. Most spacing evidence is verbal or factual recall, and how well it generalizes to multi-step physics problem solving is less established.

## 5. AI tutoring efficacy, 2023–2026

### Takeaway
The evidence has split sharply by design. **Pedagogically structured AI tutors** can match or beat strong human instruction:
- Harvard physics: 0.73–1.3 SD over active learning.
- World Bank Nigeria: 0.31 SD.
- LearnLM in UK schools: at least as good as human tutors.
- Tutor CoPilot: +4 percentage points in mastery.

**Unguarded answer-giving chatbots** raise performance while the tool is available but harm learning once it is removed (Bastani: −17% on the exam). Using an LLM as a substitute for your own work lowers understanding, while using it as a complement raises it. Real-world deployment at scale (Khanmigo in Tennessee) shows much smaller effects (≈ 0.02–0.08 SD), partly because students often send bare answers instead of engaging. Pooled meta-analyses of "ChatGPT effects" are of poor quality: at least one prominent one has been **retracted**.

### Cited Findings
- **Harvard physics AI tutor RCT (Kestin, Miller, Klales et al., Scientific Reports, June 2025; preprint 2024).**
  - N = 194 in Harvard's Physical Sciences 2 (the largest intro physics class), Fall 2023, crossover design over two weeks.
  - The AI tutor at home was compared with an in-class *active learning* lesson (not a passive lecture) — [Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6); [ERCT summary](https://erctpapers.com/papers/47-kestin-et-al-2025-ai-tutoring-active-learning.html).
  - Results: median learning gains more than doubled. The linear-regression effect size was 0.63, which the authors call an underestimate due to ceiling effects. Quantile regression gave **0.73–1.3 SD** (p < 10⁻⁸). Median time on task was 49 min versus the 60-min class. Time spent did not correlate with post-test score. Students reported more engagement and motivation, and 83% said the AI tutor's explanations were as good as or better than human instructors' — [Research Square preprint](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-4243877/v1); [preprint PDF](https://projects.panickssery.com/docs/kestin-2024-ai_tutoring_harvard_physics.pdf).
  - **Design principles** (preprint wording): "(i) facilitating active learning, (ii) managing cognitive load, (iii) promoting a growth mindset, (iv) scaffolding content, (v) ensuring accuracy of information and feedback, (vi) delivering such feedback and information in a targeted and timely fashion and (vii) allowing for self-pacing."
  - How the principles were implemented: (i)–(iii) through the system prompt. The authors found "a system prompt could not reliably provide enough structure to scaffold problems with multiple parts," so the platform walked students sequentially through each part of each problem. To prevent hallucination they "avoided relying solely on GPT-4 to generate solutions" and "enriched our prompts with comprehensive, step-by-step answers" written by instructors — [preprint PDF](https://projects.panickssery.com/docs/kestin-2024-ai_tutoring_harvard_physics.pdf).
  - Limitations: only immediate post-tests, custom lesson-specific assessments, not independently run, and no pre-registration found — [ERCT summary](https://erctpapers.com/papers/47-kestin-et-al-2025-ai-tutoring-active-learning.html); [ETC Journal review](https://etcjournal.com/2025/11/10/review-of-kestin-et-al-s-june-2025-harvard-study-on-ai-tutoring/). Also noted: Harvard students are a highly selected population, and the gains were measured over two weeks.
- **Bastani et al. (PNAS, June 2025), "Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning."**
  - About 1,000 Turkish high-school math students. Arms: GPT Base (ChatGPT-like), GPT Tutor (teacher-designed hints, no answers) and control.
  - Practice performance: GPT Base **+48%**, GPT Tutor **+127%** versus control.
  - Exam without AI access: GPT Base **−17%** versus control (statistically significant). GPT Tutor largely mitigated the harm.
  - Mechanism: students used the base model as a crutch to get answers.
  - [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/); [author PDF](https://hamsabastani.github.io/education_llm.pdf); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4895486). A **correction** was published — [PNAS correction](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2518204122) (its content was not retrieved; check before citing exact figures).
- **World Bank Nigeria (De Simone et al.).** RCT of Microsoft Copilot (GPT-4) after-school tutoring for first-year senior secondary students in English over 6 weeks. Effect of **0.31 SD** on a curriculum-aligned assessment, equivalent to about 1.5–2 years of business-as-usual schooling, and among the most cost-effective education interventions. Largest effects for female students and higher-baseline students. The program included teacher facilitation and structured prompts [RCT, working paper] — [World Bank blog](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/From-chalkboards-to-chatbots-Transforming-learning-in-Nigeria); [VoxDev](https://voxdev.org/topic/education/how-ai-tutors-improved-learning-nigeria); [7 lessons](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/From-chalkboards-to-chatbots-in-Nigeria). Flag: English, not physics; the "2 years" conversion depends on assumptions about business-as-usual learning rates.
- **Stanford Tutor CoPilot (Wang, Demszky et al. 2024).** First RCT of AI-supported *human* live tutoring: more than 700 tutors and 1,000 students from underserved communities, math. Students of tutors given CoPilot were **4 pp more likely to master topics** (p < 0.01), and **9 pp** for students of lower-rated tutors. Cost about $20 per tutor per year. Tutors with CoPilot asked more guiding questions and were less likely to give away answers [RCT, working paper] — [Stanford SCALE](https://scale.stanford.edu/publications/tutor-copilot-human-ai-approach-scaling-real-time-expertise); [NSSA PDF](https://nssa.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/Tutor_CoPilot.pdf); [arXiv 2410.03017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03017v1).
- **LearnLM (Google DeepMind).**
  - Dec 2024 tech report: expert pedagogy raters preferred LearnLM over GPT-4o by an average preference strength of 31%, over Claude 3.5 by 11%, and over Gemini 1.5 Pro by 13%. Raters compared role-played tutoring conversations on a −3 to +3 scale [INDUSTRY, not a learning-outcome study] — [arXiv 2412.16429](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.16429v1).
  - Nov 2025 exploratory RCT with Eedi: N = 165 students in 5 UK secondary schools, math. LearnLM drafted messages that were supervised by 17 expert tutors. **76.4% of drafts were approved with zero or minimal edits.** LearnLM-supported students performed at least as well as human-tutored students on every outcome, and were **5.5 pp more likely to solve novel problems on later topics** (66.2% vs 60.7%). Tutors said LearnLM was strong at drafting Socratic questions [RCT, exploratory, industry-authored, human-in-the-loop] — [LearnLM Nov 2025 PDF](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/LearnLM/learnLM_nov25.pdf).
- **Khanmigo.**
  - Cluster-randomized trial (Oreopoulos & Low, NBER w35620, Aug 2026): 18 Tennessee middle schools, 6,902 student-term observations. Effects were **0.020 SD (Year 1, not significant)** and **0.084 SD (Year 2, SE 0.041)**, pooled about 1.26 national percentile ranks per term, at about $15 per student per year.
  - Only 14.5% of student messages contained math questions or reasoning, while 39.4% were bare answers. The study cannot isolate the tutor's marginal contribution from the broader Khan Academy program [RCT, working paper, not peer-reviewed] — [WINS summary](https://www.winssolutions.org/khanmigo-ai-tutoring-two-year-trial/).
  - A small undergraduate physics study (N = 69, Lunar Phases Concept Inventory) found **no significant difference** between Khanmigo and Google search, although students liked Khanmigo — [J. Teaching and Learning 2025](https://jtl.uwindsor.ca/index.php/jtl/article/view/10052).
  - Khan Academy's own efficacy posts — [Khan blog Nov 2024](https://blog.khanacademy.org/khan-academy-efficacy-results-november-2024/) [INDUSTRY].
- **Substitute versus complement (Lehmann, Cornelius & Sting 2024).** Two pre-registered lab experiments found no *overall* LLM effect on learning. Students who used the LLM to *substitute* for work (for example, generating solutions) covered more topics but understood each less. Students who used it to *complement* their work (asking for explanations) understood more. LLMs **widened the gap** between low- and high-prior-knowledge students [RCT/lab, WP] — [arXiv 2409.09047](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4941259).
- **ChatGPT meta-analyses (contested).**
  - The widely shared Wang & Fan (2025) meta-analysis in *Humanities & Social Sciences Communications* (51 studies, g = 0.867 on learning performance) is now marked **RETRACTED** — [Nature HSSC](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-04787-y).
  - Other meta-analyses report g = 0.577 (37 studies, [Liu 2025, JCAL](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcal.70096)) and g = 1.14 (66 studies, [ScienceDirect 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666920X25001766)).
  - A 2026 meta-analysis of 35 experimental studies — [Nature HSSC 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-07019-z) — could not be retrieved.
  - Flag: these pooled estimates mostly combine short, small, often unblinded studies that measure performance *with* the AI present. Bastani's design shows that setup can badly overstate learning. Treat the pooled numbers as unreliable upper bounds.

### Inferences
- **The Kestin recipe fits AetherLab closely.** Physics, a step-by-step scaffold enforced by the platform rather than the prompt, and instructor-verified step-by-step solutions injected to prevent hallucination. AetherLab's numeric ground truth and physics verifier serve the same purpose as Kestin's pre-written solutions. Keep the tutor chat anchored to the verified derivation and solver output rather than letting it solve freely.
- **Default the tutor to hints, not answers** (Bastani's GPT Tutor versus GPT Base). Reveal answers only after a genuine attempt, or behind a deliberate "show solution" action. Log and nudge when a student repeatedly jumps straight to the answer.
- **Engagement design matters more than model quality at scale.** Khanmigo's weak field effects, with 39% bare-answer messages, show that a strong model does not help if students do not engage in reasoning. Build forced-reasoning moments (predict, explain, fill in the step) into the flow instead of relying on an open chat box.
- **Human-in-the-loop for educators.** LearnLM's 76% no-edit approval rate under supervision, and Tutor CoPilot's biggest gains for weaker tutors, suggest a teacher-review mode where educators approve, edit or lock generated lessons and quizzes before students see them.
- **Watch for widening gaps.** Lehmann et al. found LLMs can widen prior-knowledge gaps. Adaptive scaffolding (more worked examples and pre-training for novices, less for experts) is the evidence-based counter.
- **Be modest in efficacy claims.** The best-controlled large field trial (Khanmigo, 0.02–0.08 SD) is far below the lab and short-term RCTs (0.3–1.3 SD). A realistic product claim is "designed from the Kestin / Bastani principles", not "doubles learning".

### Gaps
- No independent replication of Kestin et al. at another institution was found. It remains a single-site, two-week, non-pre-registered study.
- Content of the Bastani PNAS correction was not retrieved.
- Nigeria results are for English, not physics or STEM. I found no large RCT of an AI tutor specifically for high-school physics.
- I found no rigorous head-to-head RCT of "Socratic" versus "direct explanation" LLM tutors that holds content constant. Bastani (hints versus answers) and Tutor CoPilot (guiding questions) are the closest proxies.
- The Khan Academy study reportedly run Oct 2025–Apr 2026 with a "six-percentage-point improvement" appeared only in a search snippet and could not be verified; do not cite it.

## 6. Teacher needs: what educators want from AI tools

### Takeaway
Teacher adoption is growing but uneven, and it is driven mainly by time savings (about 5.9 hours per week among weekly users). The recurring needs are accuracy, alignment to standards and curriculum, the ability to refine or edit outputs, and guidance or training, which lags adoption. Rigorous evidence on what teachers want specifically from AI *physics* tools is thin.

### Cited Findings
- **RAND, 2023–24 school year.** 25% of teachers used AI tools for instructional planning or teaching. Science and ELA teachers were about twice as likely as math or elementary teachers to use them. Teachers and principals in higher-poverty schools were less likely to use AI — [RAND RRA134-25](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA134-25.html).
- **RAND, 2025.** AI use in schools is rising quickly, but teacher professional development, student training and school and district policy lag behind — [RAND RRA4180-1](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4180-1.html); [districts training teachers on AI, RRA956-31](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA956-31.html).
- **RAND on instructional materials.** Teachers struggle to access and implement high-quality, standards-aligned materials tailored to their students — [Atlas summary of RAND, July 2025](https://www.onatlas.com/blog/curriculum-reform-rand-atlas) (secondary summary; flag). An earlier RAND report covers using AI tools in K-12 classrooms — [RAND RRA956-21 PDF](https://www.rand.org/content/dam/rand/pubs/research_reports/RRA900/RRA956-21/RAND_RRA956-21.pdf).
- **Gallup–Walton Family Foundation (2,232 US public K-12 teachers, Mar–Apr 2025).** 32% use AI at least weekly, 28% infrequently and 40% not at all. Weekly users report saving 5.9 hours per week, about six weeks per year. Across nine task types, 60–84% of users say AI saves time and 7% or fewer say it costs time — [Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/691967/three-teachers-weekly-saving-six-weeks-year.aspx); [Walton](https://www.waltonfamilyfoundation.org/the-ai-dividend-new-survey-shows-ai-is-helping-teachers-reclaim-valuable-time). Critics note these are self-reported time savings, not observed workflows — [Medium critique](https://medium.com/chalkdust-silicon/the-walton-gallup-ai-report-deserves-context-not-just-headlines-56164a7303a3).
- **Tutor CoPilot and LearnLM** show that AI support built for educators, where the educator keeps control, can improve instruction and even teach tutors new pedagogical moves — [Tutor CoPilot](https://scale.stanford.edu/publications/tutor-copilot-human-ai-approach-scaling-real-time-expertise); [LearnLM Nov 2025](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/LearnLM/learnLM_nov25.pdf).

### Inferences
- **What educators need in AetherLab:**
  - Edit and approve generated lessons, quizzes and derivations before sharing (control).
  - Tag outputs to curriculum or standards (for example AP Physics 1/2 units, IB, NGSS, A-level) and constrain notation and level (alignment).
  - Visible verification status for every numeric claim and derivation step (accuracy).
  - Exportable quizzes with answer keys and misconception-coded distractors, plus class-level results (assessment).
- **Time savings are the adoption hook, and learning outcomes are the retention hook.** Generating a ready-to-teach concept-question set, a PhET-style guided slider activity and a study guide in one pass targets the tasks where teachers report the biggest AI time savings.
- **Equity.** Adoption is lower in higher-poverty schools, so an offline-capable desktop app with low per-seat cost may fit those settings, but that is speculative.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed survey specifically of *physics* teachers' requirements for AI explainer or animation tools.
- I found no evidence on how teachers judge the accuracy of AI-generated physics animations, or on what verification signals they trust.
- Survey evidence on teacher needs (accuracy, alignment) is mostly secondary summaries or self-report; the primary RAND 2025 instrument text was not retrieved in this session.
